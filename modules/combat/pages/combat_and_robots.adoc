= Robot Combat

Robot combat is a legacy section.
Robots are just another persona in the tactical combat rules.
There are more similarities than differences.

.*Robot Combat Considerations*
* *Free Will*
** Combat can be an internal computational struggle.
** xref:uncombat:robot_free_will.adoc[Free Will,window=_blank, rel="noopener noreferrer"] rolls may be required.
* *Fabricator*
** Often robots cannot harm their fabricator genus (e.g., canine. or feline)
** While this is not absolute it may task Free Will.
* *Ramming*
** All robots can ram into targets.
** Ramming 0 makes ramming difficult for the robot.
** Ramming 4 makes the robot a ramming machine
* *Defence*
** Robot Defence is 700 unless hardened by peripherals.
* *Attack Bonuses*
** Skilled Bonus is used for baked-in weapons designed for combat.
** Raw Bonus is used for misused peripherals.
** A built-in gun is a weapon; a built-in stapler used to shoot at targets is a misused peripheral.
* *Damage*
** Robots lose hit points the same way other personas do.
** Electrical and disintegration attacks inflict double damage.
* *Hit Points*
** A robot is neither destroyed nor unconscious a 0 HPS.
** But they do deteriorate, losing attributes and peripherals.


== Fabricator
The relationship between a robot and her fabricator type is fluid at the best of times.
There are no https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov[Asimovian] 3 rules in EXP.
Robots cannot easily engage in combat with their fabricator type.
For a robot to intentionally harm a persona from her fabricator type would require a xref:uncombat:robot_free_will.adoc[Control Factor,window=_blank, rel="noopener noreferrer"] Roll.

A robot may destroy an entire expedition and leave the anthro of her fabricator type unharmed.
Robots cannot be easily convinced or easily tricked to attack their fabricator type.
Not harming one’s fabricator type is a soft rule.
The rule represents more of an internal struggle than a priority command.

The referee should not weaponize fabricator type against robot personas.
A referee persona robot may allow personas from her fabricator type to smash her into bits.
A player persona robot can run away, immobilize attackers, or even fight back if a control factor roll is successful.

== Robotic Commands
Priority commands are specialized attacks made by mechanics with robot skills.
Strangely worded commands can *immobilize a robot* by creating logical dilemmas within its reasoning circuitry.
Priority commands are the robot’s version of a mental attack on the robot’s circuitry.

The robot will suffer debilitating effects for the length of time 1 to 100 units in duration.
While this duration appears short, this is a tactical combat maneuver, and 1d100 units a lot of combat time.
Priority commands may slow the robot to move at half speed, ignore a particular weapon, or forget how to turn left.
The referee and the players negotiate the effect of the priority command.

include::combat:partial$robot_system_damage.adoc[]

The higher the robot’s control factor, the more difficult it is for a priority command to be successful.
The Difficulty (DD) of a priority command is 128 plus the robot’s CF.
Referees will often have priority commands prepared for their robot referee personas.
Scenario-related priority commands can come along with clues found in the milieu of the game.

== Robot Override
Robotic overrides are specialized attacks made by mechanics with robot skills.
Robotic overrides can involve strangely worded commands, and mechanical interventions may allow the mechanic to *control the robot*.
There are even specialized artifacts that assist robot override.

Robotic overrides are like a mental attack on the robot’s malfunctioning free will.
Successful robot override turns temporarily turns control of the robot over to the attacking mechanic.
A robotic override would be devastating to a player persona robot.
Control lasts 1-100 minutes (1d100).
The override can end abruptly, freeing the robot to exact revenge.
The robotic override difficulty  is 168 plus the robot’s Control Factor.

Robotic overrides on powerful plot-related robots may require missions to acquire the needed tech and knowledge.

== Ramming
A robot may smash itself into a target with the full intent of inflicting damage.
Ramming requires the robot and target to cross paths, and a successful attack roll.
If the robot successfully rams a target she may damage herself as well as her target.

=== Ramming Level
All robots come with Ramming 0.
This is the lowest level of Ramming and requires a Free Will check, uses the Strike Raw Bonus and inflicts reciprocal damage on the robot.

=== Attack Roll
If the robot and the target cross each other’s paths on the hex mat, the player may attempt to ram.
If a ram attempt is allowed the player must meet, or beat, the target's Defence to score a hit.
This means that if the target is faster than, and can avoid, the robot the persona cannot ram the target.
If this cannot be resolved on the playing mat a xref:uncombat:sphincter_rolls.adoc[Sphincter Roll,window=_blank, rel="noopener noreferrer"] may be required.

The Ramming Level of the robot determines whether the player used the Skilled or Raw Strike attack bonus.

=== Ramming Speed
The faster the robot is going, the more ramming damage it delivers.
The relative speed of the target and the robot directly determines how much damage is delivered.
Relative speed sounds boring, so we call it Ramming Speed instead.

If the target is moving away from the robot the target's speed is subtracted from the robot's speed.
If the target and robot are traveling head on the two speeds are added.
If the target is moving away faster than the robot, the robot cannot catch her to ram.

.*Ramming Speed*
. *Robot Move - Target Move*
.. Most common situation
.. Target is trying to avoid getting rammed

Consider a robot is moving at 12 h/u, and a target moving at 3 h/u.
Typically the target is trying to avoid the ram and the Ramming Speed is 9 h/u.
If the target were charging the robot, the Ramming Speed would be 15 h/u.

There are no rules for angled attacks.
That kind of arithmetic hurts the brains of even hardened tactical combat players.
A stationary target has a move of 0 h/u, and the robot has a ramming speed equal to its move.

=== Damage Roll
The damage roll determines the base damage of the collision before size adjustments.
Do not add the Strike Force Bonus to ramming damage.
Add the surface’s speed-related damage dice for each complete 3 h/u of Ramming Speed.
For example, 8 h/u provides 2 additional dice.
The base damage die is always included after a successful ram.

Consider a robot with a flat ramming surface (1d4 + 1d4 per 3 h/u).
With a Ramming Speed of 9 h/u the player rolls 1d4 plus 3d4 (4d4) Hit Points for the damage roll.
With a Ramming Speed of 15 h/u the player would roll 6d4 Hit Points for the damage roll.

Consider a robot with a sharp point ramming surface (1d10 + 1d8 per 3 h/u).
At 9 h/u the damage roll would be 1d10 + 3d8, and at 15 h/u it would be 1d10 + 5d8 (ouch!).

=== Size Matters
In most cases the robot will deliver, and take, the damage roll from above.
Robot ramming damages the robot.
How much that damage is depends on the relative sizes of the targets.
Robots with a high Ramming Level know how to ram without damaging themselves.

In a simple world the ramming robot and the target take the same damage.
However we are not simple people using the Tactical Combat System.
Consider being hit by a bicycle vs a dump truck.
The bigger you are, the harder they fall.

include::combat:partial$ramming_size_differential_web.adoc[]

A dash means no size adjustment: both participants take the original damage roll.
Otherwise, multiply the damage received by the smaller participant by the listed factor and divide the damage received by the larger participant by that factor.
Calculate both amounts from the original damage roll, not from each other’s adjusted damage.
At Ramming 3 or 4, the attacking robot takes no reciprocal damage, regardless of the size comparison.

For a smaller robot ramming a larger target the damage multiplier reduces the damage roll delivered, and increases the reciprocal damage taken.
So a Tiny robot ramming a Tiny robot with a 12 damage roll would deliver 12 HPS of damage to the target and take 12 HPS of damage themselves.
The same Tiny robot with a damage roll of 12 HPS ramming a Large target would deliver 6 HPS of damage and take 24 HPS of damage.
The reciprocal damage can be avoided if the robot has a high enough Ramming level.

For a larger robot ramming a smaller target the damage multiplier increases the damage delivered and decreases the reciprocal damage taken.
So a Large robot ramming a Small target with a damage roll of 12 would inflict 24 HPS of damage and take 6 HPS of damage.
The reciprocal damage can be avoided if the robot has a high enough Ramming level.

Damage is a way for robots to earn EXPS and gain experience Levels.
See xref:robots:experience_and_levels.adoc[Robot Vocation,window=_blank, rel="noopener noreferrer"].

=== Crush Multiplier
NOTE: This obsolete calculation is retained for historical reference only. Use the Size Matters table above instead; do not combine the two methods.

Reciprocation was formerly called the Crush Multiplier, and involved maths.
It is shown here for no particular reason.
If the robot and the target have the same wate description, the crush multiplier does not apply.
So a medium-sized robot does not get a crush multiplier against a medium-sized target.

.*Ramming Crush Multiplier*
* *Robot wate (kg) / target wate (kg)*
** Maximum is 4 times
** Minimum is 1/4 times

If a 120 kg robot rams a 40 kg target, the player triples the damage.
In this case, 24 HPS of damage would become 72 HPS of damage.
If a 40 kg robot were to ram a 120 kg target, the player decreases the damage 3 times.
In this case, 24 HPS of damage would become 8 HPS of damage.
If both the robot and the target were medium-sized, the crush multiplier would not apply.
